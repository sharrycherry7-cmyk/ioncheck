IonCheck | Water quality and cation identification

A practical guide to identifying metal ions and understanding how dissolved metals can affect drinking water, human health and ecosystems.

What happens in each test?

Use the observation, identify the product, then explain its solubility.

Fe³⁺

Iron(III) ions

Iron in the +3 oxidation state

Test 1 · Add a few drops of NaOH
Observation: reddish-brown precipitateIron(III) ions react with hydroxide ions to form iron(III) hydroxide.
Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)

Why? Most hydroxides are insoluble. Fe(OH)₃ is insoluble in water, so it forms a solid precipitate. The reddish-brown colour supports the identification of Fe³⁺.

Test 2 · Add a few drops, then excess NH₃
Observation: reddish-brown precipitate remainsThe precipitate does not dissolve when excess ammonia is added.
NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)

Why? Ammonia reacts with water to produce some OH⁻ ions. These form Fe(OH)₃, which remains insoluble in excess ammonia. No soluble ammonia complex forms under these test conditions.

Cu²⁺

Copper(II) ions

Copper in the +2 oxidation state

Test 1 · Add a few drops of NaOH
Observation: blue precipitateCopper(II) ions react with hydroxide ions to form copper(II) hydroxide.
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)

Why? Cu(OH)₂ is an insoluble hydroxide, so a blue solid forms. Its characteristic colour helps distinguish Cu²⁺ from Fe³⁺.

Test 2 · Add a few drops, then excess NH₃
Observation: blue precipitate, then deep-blue solutionIn excess ammonia, the precipitate dissolves and a deep-blue solution forms.
Cu(OH)₂(s) + 4NH₃(aq) ⇌ [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)

Why? Excess NH₃ molecules act as ligands and form a soluble copper-ammonia complex ion. The deep-blue solution is characteristic evidence for Cu²⁺.

How to apply solubility rules

The rule is only useful when you connect it to the product in your reaction.

The hydroxide rule

Most hydroxides are insoluble, with important exceptions such as Group 1 hydroxides and ammonium hydroxide. Therefore, many metal ions form precipitates when OH⁻ is added.

Metal ion + OH⁻ → metal hydroxide(s)

For these examples, Fe(OH)₃ and Cu(OH)₂ are insoluble, so both form precipitates.

1
Name the ionsNaOH supplies OH⁻ ions. Aqueous ammonia also produces OH⁻ ions by reacting with water.
2
Work out the productFor example, Fe³⁺ and OH⁻ form Fe(OH)₃.
3
Apply the ruleMost hydroxides are insoluble, so Fe(OH)₃ forms a solid.
4
Link to the observationState that the insoluble product explains the precipitate and include its observed colour.
Assessment tip: Avoid writing only “a precipitate forms because of the solubility rules.” Name the actual compound, state whether it is soluble, and connect that to the observation.

Metal ions in drinking water and the environment

Different metals enter water through natural processes and human activity. Their effects depend on the concentration, chemical form, pH and the sensitivity of organisms exposed.

Important distinction: Finding a metal ion in a qualitative-analysis test does not automatically mean the water is unsafe. A laboratory test identifies possible ions; measuring the concentration is needed to assess water quality.
Pb²⁺

Lead(II) ions

High-priority toxic contaminant

Why lead is a concernLead has no known beneficial role in the human body, and there is no known level of exposure that is completely without risk. Children and developing babies are particularly vulnerable.

How can lead enter water?

Natural sources: Weathering of lead-containing minerals can release small amounts of lead into groundwater and surface water.

Human-made sources: Lead can leach from older lead pipes, solder, brass taps and fittings. Mining, smelting, battery manufacture and battery recycling can release lead into nearby soil and waterways. Contaminated dust and soil can also be washed into drains and streams.

Effects on human health: Lead can affect the brain and nervous system, especially during childhood when the brain is developing. Exposure is associated with learning and attention difficulties and reduced intellectual development. Long-term exposure in adults can contribute to high blood pressure, cardiovascular problems and kidney damage. Lead can accumulate in bones and may be released later.

Effects on the environment: Lead does not break down into harmless substances. It can remain in soil and sediment, where plants and animals may be exposed. High concentrations can harm aquatic organisms and wildlife; contaminated organisms can transfer lead through food webs.

Why monitor it? Lead may be present without changing the taste, smell or appearance of water. Testing is therefore important when lead-containing plumbing or an industrial source is suspected.

Pb²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → PbSO₄(s)

Lead sulfate is insoluble, so sulfate ions can form a white precipitate with Pb²⁺ in a qualitative test.

Reference: WHO — Lead poisoning and health; Health New Zealand — Lead poisoning.

Al³⁺

Aluminium ions

Common in rocks and water-treatment chemicals

How can aluminium enter water?

Natural sources: Aluminium is abundant in rocks and soils. Weathering can release dissolved aluminium, particularly when soils and water are acidic.

Human-made sources: Aluminium sulfate (alum) is used as a coagulant in some water-treatment plants. Mining, refining and industrial wastewater can also release aluminium. Poorly managed waste and runoff from disturbed land may add aluminium to waterways.

Effects on human health: Most aluminium taken in through food and water is not absorbed and is eliminated. The health effects of typical drinking-water exposure are not fully established. It is inaccurate to claim that aluminium in drinking water is proven to cause Alzheimer's disease; research has not established a causal link. People with severely impaired kidney function may be more vulnerable to aluminium accumulation.

Effects on the environment: In acidic water, dissolved aluminium can become more available to aquatic organisms. It can damage fish gills and interfere with breathing and salt balance. In acidic soils it can damage plant roots, limiting water and nutrient uptake and reducing plant growth.

Why monitor it? Excess residual aluminium from treatment can cause cloudy water or deposits. Keeping treatment and filtration well controlled helps limit residual aluminium.

Al³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Al(OH)₃(s)

Aluminium hydroxide is a white, gelatinous precipitate in the usual qualitative test.

Reference: Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — Aluminium; ANZG — Aluminium in aquatic environments.

Cu²⁺

Copper(II) ions

Essential in small amounts; harmful at high concentrations

How can copper enter water?

Natural sources: Weathering of copper-bearing rocks and minerals can release copper into streams and groundwater.

Human-made sources: Corrosion of copper plumbing, especially in acidic or soft water, can raise copper levels at the tap. Mining, metal processing, electroplating and some algaecides can also release copper.

Effects on human health: Copper is an essential trace nutrient, but high concentrations in drinking water can cause nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps and diarrhoea. People with certain disorders affecting copper regulation may be more sensitive.

Effects on the environment: At elevated concentrations, copper can be toxic to fish, aquatic invertebrates and algae. It can interfere with gill function and other biological processes. Toxicity depends on water chemistry, including hardness and dissolved organic matter.

Why monitor it? Corrosion can cause copper to enter water after it leaves the treatment plant. High concentrations can also create a metallic taste and blue-green staining.

Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s)

Copper(II) hydroxide forms a blue precipitate. Excess ammonia can dissolve it to form a deep-blue complex.

Reference: Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — Copper.

Fe²⁺ / Fe³⁺

Iron(II) and iron(III) ions

Common natural minerals and corrosion products

How can iron enter water?

Natural sources: Iron minerals in rocks and soils can dissolve into groundwater. Low-oxygen groundwater often contains more dissolved Fe²⁺; when it reaches oxygenated water, some iron oxidises to Fe³⁺ and forms solid iron compounds.

Human-made sources: Corrosion of iron or steel pipes, mining, steel production and industrial discharge can add iron to water.

Effects on human health: Iron is an essential nutrient. Iron in drinking water at concentrations commonly encountered is generally more of an aesthetic and operational issue than a direct health concern. Very high amounts may cause stomach or intestinal discomfort.

Effects on the environment: Iron precipitates can coat streambeds and aquatic plants, smothering habitats and reducing light. Large iron deposits can affect aquatic organisms and alter the appearance and chemistry of water.

Why monitor it? Iron can produce reddish-brown staining, metallic taste and deposits that block pipes or filters.

Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)₃(s)

Fe(OH)₃ forms a reddish-brown precipitate. Fe²⁺ commonly forms a pale-green hydroxide precipitate that can turn brown as it oxidises.

Reference: WHO — Guidelines for drinking-water quality.

Zn²⁺

Zinc ions

Essential trace nutrient; excess can affect water quality

How can zinc enter water?

Natural sources: Weathering of zinc-bearing minerals can release zinc into surface water and groundwater.

Human-made sources: Zinc can leach from galvanised pipes, fittings and rainwater tanks. Mining, smelting, metal processing, tyre wear and industrial runoff are other possible sources.

Effects on human health: Zinc is needed for normal growth and immune function. Very high intake can cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach cramps. Long-term excessive intake can interfere with copper absorption and contribute to copper deficiency.

Effects on the environment: Excess zinc can be toxic to aquatic plants, invertebrates and fish. It can interfere with growth and enzyme function. The amount that organisms absorb depends on water chemistry and sediment conditions.

Why monitor it? High zinc levels can give water an unpleasant metallic taste or cloudy appearance and may indicate corrosion of galvanised materials.

Zn²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Zn(OH)₂(s)

Zinc hydroxide is white and can dissolve in excess hydroxide, producing a soluble zincate complex.

Reference: Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — Zinc.

Ba²⁺

Barium ions

Occurs naturally and in some industrial materials

How can barium enter water?

Natural sources: Barium can dissolve from rocks and minerals, particularly in some groundwater systems.

Human-made sources: Mining, drilling, metal and chemical industries, and disposal of barium-containing materials can release barium compounds into the environment.

Effects on human health: Barium is not an essential nutrient. Soluble barium compounds at high concentrations can affect muscles, the nervous system and the cardiovascular system. The risk depends strongly on the compound and dose; insoluble barium sulfate is much less readily absorbed.

Effects on the environment: Elevated barium can affect aquatic organisms. Barium can also bind with sulfate and carbonate to form poorly soluble minerals, which influences how much remains dissolved and available to organisms.

Why monitor it? Some barium compounds dissolve readily, so groundwater near natural mineral deposits or industrial sites may need testing.

Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)

Barium sulfate is insoluble and forms a white precipitate.

Reference: Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — Barium.

Ag⁺

Silver ions

Usually present at very low levels in natural water

How can silver enter water?

Natural sources: Small quantities may be released by weathering of silver-containing minerals.

Human-made sources: Photographic processing, electronics, metal refining, mining, some antimicrobial products and silver-containing materials can release silver into wastewater.

Effects on human health: Long-term exposure to high amounts of silver can cause argyria, a usually permanent blue-grey discolouration of skin. Typical environmental exposure is generally very low; the concern is sustained high exposure rather than ordinary trace presence.

Effects on the environment: Dissolved silver can be toxic to aquatic microorganisms and invertebrates at low concentrations. It may affect fish and other organisms, although silver can bind to chloride, sulfide and organic matter, reducing the amount freely dissolved.

Why monitor it? Silver is used in some antimicrobial technologies, so poorly controlled discharge can introduce it to wastewater and receiving environments.

Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)

Silver chloride forms a white precipitate that darkens when exposed to light. It dissolves in dilute ammonia by forming a complex ion.

Reference: Australian Drinking Water Guidelines — Silver.

Mg²⁺

Magnesium ions

Common natural mineral ion

How can magnesium enter water? Magnesium dissolves naturally from rocks such as dolomite and other minerals. It can also enter water through industrial discharges and some mineral-processing activities.

Effects on human health: Magnesium is an essential nutrient involved in muscle, nerve and bone function. Normal drinking-water levels are not generally a concern. Very high concentrations, especially from some supplements or medicinal sources, can cause diarrhoea; people with severely reduced kidney function may be less able to remove excess magnesium.

Effects on the environment: Magnesium is a normal part of freshwater chemistry and is required by organisms. Unusually high mineral concentrations can contribute to salinity and change which species thrive, although magnesium itself is not usually treated as a major freshwater pollutant.

Why monitor it? Magnesium contributes to water hardness and can react with carbonate to form scale in pipes and equipment.

Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)₂(s)

Magnesium hydroxide is a white precipitate and is insoluble in excess ammonia under the usual school test conditions.

Ca²⁺

Calcium ions

Common mineral in natural water

How can calcium enter water? Calcium is released as water passes through limestone, chalk, gypsum and other calcium-containing rocks. Construction materials, cement runoff and some industrial processes can also raise calcium levels.

Effects on human health: Calcium is an essential mineral needed for bones, teeth, muscles and nerve signalling. Calcium in ordinary drinking water is not generally considered a contaminant. Very hard water can cause scale, but hardness itself is mainly an operational and aesthetic issue.

Effects on the environment: Calcium helps buffer acidity and supports shells and skeletons in many aquatic organisms. Changes in hardness can influence how toxic some other dissolved metals are to aquatic life.

Why monitor it? Calcium contributes to water hardness and can form limescale in pipes, kettles and treatment equipment.

Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CaCO₃(s)

Calcium carbonate is insoluble and can form scale or a white precipitate when carbonate ions are present.

New Zealand drinking-water limits

Maximum acceptable values (MAVs) from the Water Services (Drinking Water Standards for New Zealand) Regulations 2022.

DeterminandMaximum acceptable valueWhy this matters
Lead0.01 mg/LLead is toxic, particularly to children's developing nervous systems.
Aluminium1 mg/LControls the permitted concentration in regulated drinking water; treatment residuals are also managed for appearance and operational reasons.
Barium1.5 mg/LLimits exposure to soluble barium compounds.
Copper2 mg/LProtects against adverse health effects from excessive copper intake.

These are legal drinking-water MAVs, not concentrations at which every person will immediately become ill. Some metals listed elsewhere on this page do not have a separate MAV in this NZ table. The values should not be applied to rivers or lakes as ecological safety limits.

Lead example: New Zealand's MAV for lead is 0.01 mg/L (10 micrograms per litre). WHO states that no level of lead exposure is known to be without harmful effects, so keeping exposure as low as reasonably practical is important.

Official source: New Zealand Legislation — Water Services (Drinking Water Standards for New Zealand) Regulations 2022.
